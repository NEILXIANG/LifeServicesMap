🏙️ About {CITY}

{CITY} sits in the southern Interior of British Columbia, at the confluence of the North Thompson and South Thompson Rivers, on the traditional territory of the Tk̓emlúps te Secwépemc (Secwepemc Nation). The city's population is roughly 98,000 (2021 Census), about 127,000 across the metro area. The climate is semi-arid: hot, dry summers (average July highs around 27–28°C) and cold winters (average January lows around -7 to -8°C), with much less precipitation than coastal BC cities like Vancouver. {CITY} is nicknamed the "Tournament Capital of Canada" thanks to its extensive sports facilities and the many amateur tournaments it hosts each year. The local economy centres on Thompson Rivers University (TRU), forestry, mining, a regional hospital hub, and tourism.

🕰️ How this city became what it is

The river came first, then trade. In 1812 a fur post was set up at the meeting of the two rivers — the beginning of the city and the source of its name (Tk̓emlúps, where the waters meet). Gold-seekers passed through in the 1860s, the railway arrived in 1885, and when the city was incorporated in 1893 it had about a thousand people. It stayed small for sixty years, then from the late 1950s grew fast as the interior's regional centre: the hospital, the university (TRU), forestry and mining, and the sports facilities built around the tournaments it hosts. The 2021 population was 97,902. Most of this city's growth, in other words, happened within living memory — including the part where it became a city of many languages.

🧡 The land you have moved onto, and a history worth knowing

{CITY} sits on the traditional territory of the Tk̓emlúps te Secwépemc. The name Tk̓emlúps means the meeting of the rivers — the confluence this whole city was built around, and the same one you can see on the map. It is part of Secwepemcúl'ecw, the traditional and unceded territory of the Secwépemc People — a nation of 17 communities whose language is Secwepemctsín. Each June the Kamloopa Powwow at Tk̓emlúps is open to everyone; going once is a normal thing for someone who lives here to do.

There is a part of that history a new resident will meet quickly, in the news, on street signs, and in conversation. The {CITY} Indian Residential School operated here from 1890 until 1978, run by the Catholic Church; at its peak it was the largest school in Canada's residential school system, with up to 500 children there at a time. Children were taken from their families and punished for speaking their own languages. In May 2021 the Nation announced preliminary findings from a ground-penetrating radar survey of the grounds, and the news travelled around the world.

You will see orange shirts here, especially around September 30, the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation. Wearing one is a normal thing for anyone living here to do.

If you would like to learn properly rather than from headlines, the Secwepemc Museum & Heritage Park is run by the Nation itself and is the right place to start. For anyone affected by residential schools, the KUU-US Crisis Line answers 24 hours for Indigenous people across BC: 1-800-588-8717. The Indian Residential School Survivors and Family Crisis Line (1-866-925-4419) and Hope for Wellness (1-855-242-3310) also answer 24 hours.

The Nation calls the children who did not come home Le Estcwicwéy̓ — “the missing”. At memorials, follow the Nation’s own protocols.

🏡 If you have lived here for years

ARCH is the City’s recreation subsidy: income-qualified residents get up to $400 a year towards swimming, gym and programmes, paying 15% themselves, and apply through a referring agency. ARCH members can also buy KamPASS, a low-cost transit pass (sold at the {CITY} Museum). With a library card, the library’s Community Pass lends a free family pass for a week — pools, the TCC, the BC Wildlife Park, the science centre and the Paramount. When it snows, Snow Angels volunteers clear walks for seniors and people with limited mobility — ask for help, or sign up to give it. The municipal election is on October 17, 2026; voting needs Canadian citizenship. For a parent who is getting older, see the card on caring for an older relative.

🧳 You are not the first to arrive here

This city's immigration history is longer than its streetscape suggests. Knowing it is not nostalgia — what you are doing now, many people have already done, and what they left behind is still here.

Between 1881 and 1885, more than 15,000 Chinese labourers came to Canada to build the Canadian Pacific Railway. By the end of 1882, of the 9,000 railway workers in British Columbia, 6,500 were Chinese. They were paid between a third and a half of what white workers earned, and out of that paid for their own food and gear, while being given the most dangerous work — including handling nitroglycerin. The line they built runs through this valley.

When the railway was finished, people stayed. By 1890 the Chinese population here was over 400, with its own quarter west of First Avenue. The Chinese Cemetery on Lombard Street was in use by 1887 and closed to burials in 1979; it is one of the largest and oldest intact Chinese cemeteries in Canada, with about 125 plots, over fifty of which were disinterred — Guangdong practice was secondary burial, the bones collected after seven to ten years and shipped home to the family plot. Permanent headstones were first set here in 1927. That year is a turning point you can read in stone: from "one day I will go back" to "this is where I live."

Meanwhile the country's laws said otherwise. A head tax on Chinese immigrants was imposed in 1885 and raised twice, in 1902 and 1903. From 1 July 1923 the Chinese Immigration Act barred Chinese immigration almost entirely, and was not repealed until 1947. On 22 June 2006 the Prime Minister apologised in Parliament for the head tax. These were national laws, not local ones.

In 1942 about 22,000 people of Japanese descent were forcibly uprooted from the BC coast (published figures range from 21,000 to 23,000 depending on what is counted); more than three quarters of them were Canadian citizens. Men were sent to build roads: seventeen camps ran along the Yellowhead highway up to Blue River, the southernmost of them in the North Thompson valley north of this city. The first group arrived in February 1942 and the project closed in May 1946. They were paid a little over 40 per cent of the standard wage, with room and board deducted, four armed RCMP officers were posted at each camp, and no one could leave without permission. The restrictions were not lifted until 1 April 1949. On 22 September 1988 the federal government signed the redress agreement.

That history left something here you can walk into today: the Japanese Canadian cultural centre at 160 Vernon Avenue was built in 1994, funded in part by the money from that 1988 redress. It is run by the local association formed in the mid-1970s, and its museum reopened in February 2026.

Punjabi and Sikh families settled across the BC interior with the forest industry, particularly after the 1950s; the city has a gurdwara and an active Sikh cultural society today.

And one more thing worth knowing on your first week. Between 1979 and 1982 large numbers of Southeast Asian refugees — the "boat people" — arrived in Canada. In March 1980 a Vietnamese and Immigrants' Community Centre opened here, and the local Chinese associations carried the reception work under a two-year mandate. On 20 January 1982 the organisation was incorporated, and in 1985 it took the name it still has: {CITY} Immigrant Services. Which means the agency that will help you today was founded for Vietnamese refugees over forty years ago — and the people who helped build it were the descendants of the men who built the railway a century before that.

The thread has never broken. Seven Syrian families were resettled here in 2015 and 2016, more than forty people by that June. Ukrainians began arriving after 2022, under a federal emergency travel programme rather than as refugees.

You are on this list. One day it will be your turn to help whoever arrives next.

🧭 Your First Stop: Local Settlement Services

If you've just arrived and have time for exactly one thing, make it {CITY} Immigrant Services (448 Tranquille Road; 778-470-6101 or 1-866-672-0855; Monday–Friday 8:30–16:30; find it under Category → Gov/Immigration on this map). It is the local settlement agency, its services are free, and they include:

Free English classes (LINC) — for residents 17 and older who are permanent residents, refugees, or on a confirmed path to permanent residency; classes run Monday to Thursday with morning, afternoon and evening sections, plus home study and conversation circles, and free preschool childcare during class for eligible participants. Settlement and integration support — help understanding forms, government letters and appointment processes. Employment services — résumé review, mock interviews, mentorship matching. SWIS (Settlement Workers in Schools) — a free service for resident families in the {SD} district that helps children settle into school, supports parents at meetings, assists with forms, and can arrange interpretation. Interpretation and translation — just ask.

Two other high-value doors: the public library (Category → Libraries) gives you a free card, public computers and printing, children's programmes and adult conversation circles — one of the cheapest footholds a resident has; and 2-1-1, where you can describe your situation in your own language and be referred to the right local agency.

🚌 Living Here Without a Car

{CITY} is spread out and genuinely hilly. Downtown, North Shore, Sahali, Aberdeen, Valleyview, Brocklehurst and Westsyde are not close together, and the grades are steep — residents routinely underestimate how hard a "only two kilometres" walk turns out to be. {TRANSIT} runs the local buses (the 🚌 Transit panel in this tool holds every regular route and stop and works offline), with reduced fares for students and seniors. The city's KamPASS affordable-transit programme provides subsidised passes for low-income residents; it shares one application form with the ARCH (Affordable Recreation for Community Health) programme and is submitted through a designated referring agency. ARCH itself lets eligible low-income residents use city pools, fitness and recreation facilities at a steep discount. For a family that has just landed without steady income, those two are the first local benefits worth asking about. Allow extra time waiting for a bus in winter: icy grades cause delays, and salted pavement is slippery.

🎓 Studying at TRU, or Coming With Someone Who Does

Thompson Rivers University (TRU) is the city's largest educational institution and, for many international students, the first community they land in. Campuses normally have an international student office (permits, insurance, orientation), academic advising and a writing centre, free counselling, a students' union extended health and dental plan, and country and culture clubs — most of it already covered by tuition, so use it. The campus sits in the Sahali area with several bus routes serving it; rental demand around campus tightens sharply before each term, so start looking early. Work-hour limits, the insurance handover and post-graduation permits are covered in "For International Students" above — always check your international office's current notice. Parents who came along with a student can contact the settlement agency above at the same time: enrolling children and arranging language support runs through exactly the same process.

🌡️ The Four Seasons Here — and Two Things Outsiders Underestimate

Summer: dry heat and wildfire smoke. Daytime highs regularly pass 30°C and the air is very dry, which feels nothing like humid heat; dehydration and sunburn are what actually catch people out, so carry water and stay out of the afternoon sun. The thing worth knowing in advance is wildfire season (roughly July to September): the BC Interior can get days of heavy smoke with air quality indexes spiking. Close windows, cut back outdoor exercise, and if you or your children have asthma or a respiratory condition, keep medication on hand and watch the official Air Quality Health Index. It isn't severe every year, but it's worth being mentally prepared for.

Winter: snow, ice, and early darkness. Winter temperatures sit below freezing for long stretches, and icy roads and slick hills are normal. Three essentials: real winter boots with a grippy sole (running shoes on ice are genuinely dangerous), layered clothing (a wicking base, an insulating middle, a windproof/waterproof shell — far more effective than one thick coat), and legal winter tires (see the rules in the previous section). Also, around the solstice it is dark here by shortly after 4pm. Low mood from the sharp drop in daylight — seasonal affective disorder — is common in northern cities. Get outside in daylight, keep moving, and talk to a doctor if it's affecting you. It's a treatable condition, not a failure of willpower.

💰 Local Money-Savers and Free Resources

Food support: the {CITY} Food Bank (171 Wilson Street, 250-376-2252) distributes food to households in need on presentation of ID for each household member; families/single parents and singles/couples attend on different days, and seniors 60+ have an earlier slot — phone or check the website for current times. Recreation and transit subsidies: ARCH and KamPASS, above. Free and cheap things to do: library programmes, city parks and trails, the free summer splash parks, and low-cost community centre classes. Second-hand: local thrift stores (Category → Thrift Stores) are the best value for furniture, winter clothing and children's gear, and will save a serious amount when you're furnishing a whole home at once. Groceries: the big chains refresh their weekly flyer deals midweek; Asian markets (Category → Asian Market) are usually distinctly cheaper for rice, flour, seasonings and certain vegetables; and most supermarkets have a house brand running 20–30% below the equivalent name brand.
